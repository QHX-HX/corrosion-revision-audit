# Language and claims checklist

The reviewer's deepest complaints are almost never about grammar; they are about
**claims that outrun the evidence**. Fix those first, then style.

## 1. Letter-to-manuscript correspondence

For every "Changes made" paragraph in the response letter, open the manuscript and
confirm the change exists. Real failures seen:

- The letter said a three-point comparison had been added to the Introduction; the
  paragraph still contained a single generic sentence.
- The letter said ref [55] was deleted; [55] was still cited.
- The letter said the correlation coefficient was removed; the abstract still said
  "a significant positive correlation".

Rule: **the letter is a claim about the files, so verify it like any other claim.**

## 2. Mechanism strength

If a mechanism was downgraded from "established" to "possible", the downgrade must reach
every surface:

- [ ] Abstract
- [ ] Results heading and text
- [ ] Discussion section titles
- [ ] Conclusions
- [ ] Highlights
- [ ] Figure captions (especially a schematic figure)
- [ ] Cover letter (the revision letter must not re-assert the old claim)
- [ ] Response letter

Vocabulary ladder, strongest to weakest:
`demonstrates` > `shows` > `indicates` > `is consistent with` > `suggests` >
`may reflect` > `cannot be excluded`.

Pick the rung the evidence supports and use it everywhere.

## 3. Significance language

- "significant" belongs to a statistical test, not to an impression.
- If no test was run, use `marked`, `pronounced`, `clear`, `larger`.
- If a test was run, quote it once (F, df, p) and reuse the values.
- Watch for "significantly" hiding in literature statements; those describe other
  authors' findings and are acceptable only if the cited work tested them.

## 4. Correlation and causality

- "correlates with" requires a coefficient; if you removed the coefficient, remove the
  word.
- With three or four concentrations and a reversal, "increases with concentration"
  is only valid over the monotonic segment; state the segment.
- Passive/agentless phrasing hides causality: "the rate was increased" invites the
  question "by what?". Prefer "Fe²⁺ increased the rate between 191 and 765 μM".

## 5. Tense and voice

- Methods and results: past tense.
- Established literature: present tense.
- Conclusions: past tense for findings, present for implications.

## 6. Hedging inventory

Keep a fixed set and reuse it: `is consistent with`, `indicates`, `suggests`,
`may be attributed to`, `cannot be excluded`, `remains to be confirmed by`.

Do not stack hedges ("may possibly suggest"). One per sentence.

## 7. Rebuttal tone

- Open with what was done, not with thanks.
- Do not say "we agree" unless you actually changed something; reviewers notice.
- Never argue with the reviewer's number if the number is right; correct the manuscript.
- Keep sentences under ~40 words; long defensive sentences read as evasion.
- No internal vocabulary in the submitted letter: "route A", "red line", "our plan",
  "TODO", Chinese annotations.

## 8. Final read-through

Read the abstract, conclusions and highlights back to back. They must tell the same
story with the same numbers and the same strength of claim. If they disagree, the
reviewer will quote the most flattering one against you.
